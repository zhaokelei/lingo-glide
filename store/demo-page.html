Machine translation

Machine translation is the use of computer systems to convert text or speech from one natural language into another without human involvement in the wording of the output.

History

Early proposals in the 1950s treated translation as a code-breaking problem and relied on bilingual dictionaries plus a small set of hand-written reordering rules.

Rule-based systems dominated until the 1980s, when statistical methods began to estimate translation probabilities directly from large collections of parallel text.

Neural models arrived in the middle of the 2010s and quickly replaced most statistical pipelines, because a single network could learn word order, agreement and idiom together.

Approaches

Statistical systems break a sentence into phrases, score each candidate phrase pair against a parallel corpus, and then search for the combination with the highest total score.

Smaller models that run on a single machine trade a little fluency for privacy and latency, and they are now practical for reading tasks on ordinary laptops.

Quality

Automatic metrics compare machine output with one or more human references and reward overlap in words and word sequences, which makes them fast but blunt.

Human evaluation remains the only reliable way to judge whether a translation reads naturally, keeps the original meaning and handles names, numbers and units correctly.

Use in everyday reading

Readers who follow foreign news, documentation or forum threads often need a rough translation immediately, and they accept a few awkward phrases in exchange for speed.

Keeping the original text next to the translation helps in that situation, because the reader can check a name, a figure or a quoted sentence against the source.

Local models change the trade-off: the text never leaves the machine, so a reader can translate a private document or an internal ticket without hesitation.
